Policy proposal refinement regarding AI-generated contributions in Git, focusing on scope definition. This is part of an ongoing discussion about establishing explicit policy against AI-generated content due to DCO compliance concerns.

Christian Couder raises an important distinction between the current Git proposal's broad prohibition of "AI content generators" output versus QEMU's narrower focus on "AI code generator" output. He notes this could create inconsistencies - for example, QEMU might still accept AI-generated logos or commit messages while Git's current wording would forbid them. The observation suggests the policy may be overreaching beyond its intended legal protections.

The email highlights a key unresolved question about policy scope boundaries, specifically whether to target all generated content or just code. Couder also raises the practical consideration of leveraging Conservancy's legal expertise, noting that since QEMU (like Git) is a Conservancy project, their legal consultation could provide valuable precedent.

This moves the discussion forward by shifting focus from the general principle of prohibition (which appears to have consensus) to the more nuanced question of precisely what should be prohibited. The distinction between "content" and "code" generators introduces new dimensions to consider, particularly around non-code contributions like documentation, artwork, and commit messages. The suggestion to consult shared legal resources could help resolve these scope questions while maintaining consistency across Conservancy projects.
