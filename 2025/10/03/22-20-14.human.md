This is a legal and policy discussion in the ongoing thread about AI-generated contributions to Git. Brian M. Carlson responds to Elijah Newren's example of an accepted AI-assisted documentation patch by doubling down on the legal risks of LLM-generated content.

Carlson argues that regardless of how carefully Newren vetted his AI-assisted documentation changes, the fundamental legal uncertainty around LLM copyright makes such contributions too risky for Git. He cites international copyright concerns (Git's global distribution means compliance with all jurisdictions is required), criminal liability possibilities beyond civil indemnification, and parallels to past open source legal crises like the SCO-Linux case. The QEMU policy is presented as a model, emphasizing that current legal ambiguity makes it impossible to honestly satisfy the DCO for AI-generated content.

The response is firm but measured, acknowledging Newren's good-faith example while maintaining that the legal landscape requires a blanket prohibition for now. Carlson positions this as a risk management decision rather than a technical judgment, with openness to revisiting if legal clarity emerges. The argument leans heavily on Software Freedom Conservancy's non-profit status and ethical obligations beyond strict legal requirements.

This keeps the thread's core tension alive: how to balance legitimate AI-assisted workflows (like Newren's documented vetting process) against unquantifiable legal risks. Carlson's stance suggests the policy discussion may need to address both the "what" (defining prohibited AI use) and the "why" (communicating the legal rationale to contributors).
