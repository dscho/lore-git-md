This is a discussion about cherry-pick tracking in Git, specifically addressing historical perceptions of cherry-picking as second-class compared to merging. Oswald Buddenhagen clarifies that their earlier comment about "ideological commitment" referred to Git's historical preference for merges over cherry-picks, which they believe led to underdeveloped cherry-pick tooling. 

Junio Hamano had explained that the current SHA-1 trailer format was designed for public workflows where both original and cherry-picked commits exist in the same project. Buddenhagen acknowledges this but points out that trunk-first development (where cherry-picking from main to maintenance branches is common) has become widespread, making the lack of first-class cherry-pick support surprising. 

The exchange suggests a possible misalignment between Git's original design assumptions and modern development practices, with Buddenhagen observing that even if core maintainers now welcome better cherry-pick support, past perceptions may have slowed its development. The discussion doesn't propose new technical solutions but rather examines the cultural and historical context behind the current implementation.
