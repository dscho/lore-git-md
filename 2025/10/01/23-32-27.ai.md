This is a policy discussion email in the ongoing thread about prohibiting AI-generated contributions in Git, responding to Chuck Wolber's concerns about the proposed language.

Brian M. Carlson provides a detailed legal argument for why simply declaring AI use (as proposed by declare-ai.org) is insufficient, emphasizing the project's need to protect contributors from international copyright liability. They explain how Git's distributed nature and limited resources make it particularly vulnerable compared to corporations that can afford legal defenses. The response clarifies that the intent is indeed a near-total ban on AI-generated content, not just a warning, due to three key issues: variable quality, lack of contributor understanding, and legal risks that persist regardless of technical quality.

Carlson rejects the comparison between AI-generated patches and human contributions from unethical individuals, noting that even technically excellent AI output still carries legal risks and lacks the explainability required for open source maintenance. The email includes philosophical pushback against the inevitability of AI adoption, characterizing LLMs as fundamentally limited prediction engines prone to harmful outputs. Legal references (promissory estoppel) and jurisdictional examples (Canada vs US copyright law) ground the argument in concrete policy considerations.

The response advances the thread by solidifying the case for prohibition while addressing Wolber's concerns about clarity and fairness. It provides the legal rationale missing from earlier drafts, though operational questions remain about enforcement mechanics and potential narrow exceptions. Carlson's position as a non-US contributor adds weight to the liability arguments, reinforcing the need for globally-safe policy language in v3. The email doesn't propose specific wording changes but strengthens the conceptual foundation for the ban by framing it as necessary contributor protection rather than mere preference.
