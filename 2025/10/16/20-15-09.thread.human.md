This legal discussion began with D. Ben Knoble seeking clarification about whether Git's Signed-off-by tag carries legal weight regarding copyright or contributor agreements. Junio Hamano authoritatively explained Git's position - the tag serves as Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO) certification per the project's SubmittingPatches documentation, distinct from other projects' approaches. Collin Funk then provided detailed context about GNU's copyright assignment process, including the FSF's provision allowing contributors to retain certain rights to their code.

The discussion has now expanded with Theodore Ts'o's contribution, which brings attention to another significant aspect of FSF agreements: their indemnification clauses. Ts'o, drawing on his legal experience, warns that these clauses could expose contributors to personal financial liability and strongly advises against signing such agreements without legal counsel. He contrasts this with the Linux Kernel's DCO (which Git also uses), noting it was carefully vetted by legal professionals from multiple organizations. Ts'o also provides historical perspective on how the FSF's policies have evolved, from strict copyright assignment requirements to their current more flexible stance that sometimes accepts DCO-style contributions.

While the core question about Git's Signed-off-by interpretation was settled earlier in the thread, this latest exchange provides valuable educational context about the broader landscape of contributor agreements. The discussion highlights key differences in legal approaches between projects while reinforcing the DCO's standing through Ts'o's professional endorsement. No changes to Git's processes or documentation are proposed, but the thread now offers a comprehensive comparison of models and their implications for contributors.
