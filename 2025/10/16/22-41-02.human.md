This is a security-focused critique of the Message-ID tracking proposal for Git commits. Brian M. Carlson raises several concerns about automatically embedding email Message-IDs into commit headers as proposed in James Bottomley's patch series.

Carlson argues that Message-IDs present multiple security and content risks when stored in commits. Unlike commit messages which are visible during patch application, Message-IDs are often hidden in email clients and could contain problematic content (profanity, malware test strings) or reveal sensitive information through hostnames. They also create a persistent tracking mechanism that could compromise contributor privacy.

The critique draws parallels to known abuse patterns in Git history (like extreme timestamp values) and warns that Message-IDs would become another attack surface. While the original patch aimed to help kernel workflow tracking, Carlson suggests the automatic, invisible nature of header storage could have unintended consequences than alternatives like Git notes or specialized headers.

This email adds security considerations to the ongoing discussion about Message-ID storage approaches, favoring solutions that give users more visibility and control over what metadata gets permanently recorded in commits. The concerns appear well-reasoned and grounded in real-world abuse patterns observed in distributed version control systems.
