This is a policy discussion email in the ongoing AI contribution policy thread, with Christian Couder responding to legal concerns raised by Brian M. Carlson. The email challenges the premise that all AI-generated content should be banned due to copyright uncertainty, using Elijah Newren's previously accepted AI-assisted documentation as a concrete counterexample.

Couder argues that the legal landscape is more nuanced than a blanket prohibition would suggest. He points out that small, human-reviewed changes (like Newren's documentation patches) likely don't pose copyright issues, just as we accept some risk with human contributions that might contain proprietary code. The email questions whether Git needs to comply with every country's laws, suggesting the project could reasonably limit its legal considerations to countries hosting official mirrors and the US (where the Conservancy is based).

The response highlights practical concerns with Carlson's strict position, noting that many common activities (like translation tools using LLMs) would become problematic under a broad ban. Couder suggests the QEMU-style prohibition may be overkill, as existing review processes can catch problematic contributions regardless of their origin. The email maintains that some degree of risk acceptance is inevitable in open source development.

This continues the thread's central tension between legal risk mitigation and practical policy application. Couder's position leans toward trusting the community's review mechanisms rather than implementing sweeping prohibitions, while acknowledging the discussion itself is valuable for raising awareness of the issues.
