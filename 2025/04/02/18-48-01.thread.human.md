The discussion about standardizing change IDs has reached a critical point, with clear divisions emerging between different tools' approaches. Elijah Newren's latest contribution brings concrete evidence from Gerrit-based projects where identical change IDs across branches are intentionally used to track backports in long-term support (LTS) workflows. This directly contrasts with Jujutsu's model where each change ID must be unique.

We're seeing three main camps: those who view change IDs as lineage markers (supporting duplicates for tracking), those who treat them as unique review units, and those proposing hybrid solutions. The practical challenges are significant - Gerrit's approach creates command-line ambiguities (like which version `git switch` should pick), while Jujutsu's model breaks existing backport tracking workflows. Cherry-pick trailers, often suggested as an alternative, prove problematic in practice with complex chains and dead ends.

Newren proposes an interesting middle path: treating non-unique change IDs like abbreviated hashes, where commands would error on ambiguity rather than guessing. This would make the behavior explicit while preserving Gerrit's tracking capabilities. The discussion now faces fundamental questions about whether to standardize on one model, support multiple approaches, or develop new disambiguation mechanisms.

Underlying all this are real-world constraints: Git's current limitations in automatically rebasing descendants, the need to support large-scale enterprise maintenance, and deeply embedded workflows that teams can't easily change. The thread has moved beyond technical implementation details to confront deeper philosophical differences about what change IDs fundamentally represent in version control systems.
