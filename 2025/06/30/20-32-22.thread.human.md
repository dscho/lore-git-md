This thread has been developing Git's policy on AI-generated contributions, with significant debate around legal risks, practical enforcement, and philosophical considerations. The discussion began with Christian Couder's v2 proposal for a hybrid approach to identifying and handling AI-generated content, but has since evolved through input from multiple key contributors.

Junio Hamano has now proposed adopting QEMU's strict AI prohibition policy nearly verbatim, marking a decisive turn in the discussion. His proposal takes a firm stance against contributions generated by tools like Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude and Llama, citing legal uncertainties around copyright and DCO compliance as the primary rationale. While allowing non-contribution uses like API research, the policy would require contributors to prove legal clarity for any AI-generated content they wish to submit - setting a high bar for exceptions.

This position aligns closely with Brian M. Carlson's expanded legal arguments about international copyright risks and criminal liability, while creating tension with Elijah Newren's precedent of accepted AI-assisted contributions and Christian Couder's more nuanced hybrid approach. The proposal also leaves open Randall Becker's legal attestation framework and some practical enforcement questions, but provides a clear baseline that prioritizes legal protection over flexibility.

The thread now faces key decisions about whether to adopt this stricter approach or seek a middle ground that accommodates both legal concerns and practical contributor workflows. With Junio's proposal on the table, the discussion is moving toward concrete policy text while still needing to resolve questions about enforcement mechanics and the threshold for suspicion of AI-generated content. The outcome will significantly impact how contributors and maintainers interact with AI tools in Git's development process.
