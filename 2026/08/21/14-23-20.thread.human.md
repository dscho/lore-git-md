This thread began as a technical patch series to replace the deprecated `utime()` function with `utimensat()` for POSIX.1-2024 compatibility, but has since become a pivotal policy discussion about AI-generated contributions in Git. The series itself is technically complete, introducing a `git_utimensat()` wrapper, replacing all `utime()` calls, and adding MinGW support with full interface compatibility. However, its progress is now blocked by a **process objection** from brian m. carlson, who cited Git’s `SubmittingPatches` policy prohibiting significant AI-generated content due to legal and ethical concerns.

The discussion has expanded to address broader questions: whether the project should accept AI-assisted contributions (even with manual verification), how to balance legal risks with practical trust in contributors, and whether the policy needs formal clarification or amendment. Junio C Hamano initially framed the legal risks as a barrier but later suggested a potential middle ground, arguing that the project might tolerate AI-assisted contributions from **trusted contributors who disclose their use of tools and demonstrate sufficient due diligence**—such as verifying the code was derived from acceptable sources like Git’s own codebase or permissively licensed projects. However, Oswald Buddenhagen’s recent reply **rejects this framing**, arguing that **AI tools inherently undermine the ability to verify code provenance**, even for trusted contributors. He contends that no amount of due diligence can fully mitigate the risk of license contamination or copyright infringement, sharpening the debate into a binary choice: maintain the strict policy (blocking the series) or accept the legal exposure of AI-generated code.

Key participants remain divided. Brian m. carlson’s objection remains procedural, blocking further consideration until the policy is resolved. Weijie Yuan’s earlier observation about prior `Assisted-by` trailers (suggesting precedent for AI-assisted contributions) was withdrawn after Junio clarified that the project’s trust in Johannes Schindelin’s due diligence resolved any inconsistency. The discussion now hinges on whether the **Project Leadership Committee (PLC) or broader community** will clarify the policy’s scope, disclosure requirements, and enforcement criteria. No formal path forward has been proposed, but the impasse suggests the need for explicit guidance—either reaffirming the prohibition or defining conditions under which AI-assisted contributions might be accepted. Until then, the `utimensat()` series remains on hold, and the broader question of AI’s role in Git contributions remains unresolved.
