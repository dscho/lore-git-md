This is a **policy discussion reply** in the ongoing thread about AI-generated contributions and the project's `SubmittingPatches` policy. Oswald Buddenhagen challenges Junio C Hamano's framing of "contributor trust" as a sufficient safeguard for AI-generated code, arguing that **AI tools inherently undermine the ability to verify code provenance**—even for trusted contributors.

Oswald's core point is that **AI tools cannot be assumed to produce original or properly licensed code**, regardless of the contributor's intent or reputation. He notes that while some AI tools now detect outright plagiarism (and disclosing AI use via trailers could provide *some* assurance), this is not a reliable safeguard: tools may fail, lack such features, or produce code that is *substantially similar* to copyrighted material without being verbatim copies. Unlike manual contributions—where a trusted contributor can reasonably verify they are copying from permissive sources (e.g., Git's own codebase or BSD-licensed projects)—AI-generated code introduces **unverifiable risks** of license contamination or copyright infringement. His argument implies that **no amount of due diligence can fully mitigate this risk**, making the policy's prohibition of AI-generated content a necessary precaution.

The email does not engage with the technical merits of the `utimensat()` series (e.g., the `git_utimensat()` wrapper or MinGW support) but instead **sharpens the policy debate** by rejecting Junio's middle-ground framing. Oswald's stance aligns with brian m. carlson's earlier objection, reinforcing the legal and ethical concerns while dismissing the idea that contributor trust alone can resolve them. This reply **raises the stakes** for the policy discussion: if even trusted contributors cannot guarantee the provenance of AI-generated code, the project may have no choice but to enforce a blanket prohibition—or accept the legal risks of allowing such contributions.

No formal proposal or path forward is suggested, but the email **narrows the discussion** to a binary choice: either the project maintains its strict policy (blocking the `utimensat()` series and similar contributions) or it accepts the legal exposure of AI-generated code. The PLC or broader community will likely need to address this tension explicitly, as Junio's earlier framing of "trust and due diligence" no longer appears sufficient to resolve the impasse.
