This is a **refactoring proposal** that advances the ongoing debate over Git’s source tree reorganization by introducing a **data-driven methodology** to identify cohesive subsystems. Michael Montalbo refines his earlier counterproposal to Junio C Hamano’s subsystem-by-subsystem approach, replacing the shared-header cohesion metric with a **dual-signal analysis** that combines **commit history patterns** (weighted by commit size) and **call-graph relationships** (derived from `cscope`). The script he provides groups 163 of Git’s 231 `libgit.a` source files into 14 directories—such as `transport/`, `index/`, and `revision/`—with cohesion scores higher than existing carved-out directories like `refs/` (3.1) or `odb/` (unscored but implied lower). The remaining 68 files, including utility helpers like `strbuf.c` and `hashmap.c`, are left ungrouped due to weak historical or call-graph signals.

The proposal’s core innovation is its **mechanical, reproducible methodology**, which avoids the arbitrariness of Patrick Steinhardt’s `lib/` directory while still enabling incremental progress. By weighting commit labels inversely to the number of files changed (a 2-file commit counts more than a 40-file sweep), the script surfaces **focused historical patterns** that reflect how contributors *actually* conceptualize subsystems. The call-graph check acts as an independent validator: 57 files fall into 12 call groups, with 5 groups fully agreeing with the directory assignments and 6 highlighting **boundary cases** (e.g., `shallow.c` grouped with `transport/` despite its `revision:` commit label). These boundary cases are presented as **actionable review targets**, not failures, offering a roadmap for refining the groupings.

Montalbo’s argument is pragmatic: the `lib/` approach’s appeal is its simplicity, but its lack of semantic clarity undermines the goal of improving discoverability. His alternative—**mining Git’s history and code relationships**—addresses Junio’s critique that `lib/` is an arbitrary intermediate layer while preserving Patrick’s "start somewhere" pragmatism. The script’s output suggests that **mechanical signals can surface meaningful groupings** without hand-picked lists, as evidenced by the "wire protocol" group (`connect.c`, `fetch-pack.c`, etc.) scoring higher than `refs/` in cohesion. The proposal also sidesteps the **newcomer discoverability** framing, instead appealing to **project consistency**: the same signals that would have flagged `odb/` before it was carved out now identify other cohesive units.

Key technical details for future summarizers:
- **Proposed directories**: 14 subsystems (`odb/`, `refs/`, `pack/`, `diff/`, `merge/`, `revision/`, `index/`, `setup/`, `convert/`, `transport/`, `notes/`, `submodule/`, `archive/`, `sequencer/`), with 163 files placed (145 by commit label, 12 by filename, 6 by override).
- **Methodology**: Dual-signal analysis combining (1) **commit history** (weighted modal "area:" labels, e.g., `diff:` or `refs:`) and (2) **call-graph relationships** (using `cscope` to identify function calls between files). The script excludes "plumbing" headers and weights commits inversely to their size to prioritize focused changes.
- **Boundary cases**: 6 call-graph groups span multiple directories (e.g., `shallow.c` in `revision/` but called by `transport/` files), flagged as review targets.
- **Ungrouped files**: 68 files (e.g., `strbuf.c`, `hashmap.c`, `alloc.c`) lack strong historical or call-graph signals, suggesting they may belong in a shared utility layer or require manual review.
- **Script**: A 200-line Python script that processes Git’s history, `Makefile`, and `cscope` output to generate the groupings. The `CHARTER` block (14 lines) maps area names to directories and is the only manual input.
- **Validation**: The commit-label and filename signals agree for 151 files, with no disagreements outside the 8 overrides, confirming the naming convention’s consistency.
