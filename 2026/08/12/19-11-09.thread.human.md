This thread is about a critical performance regression in Git v2.53 that causes quadratic-time slowdowns in `git rev-parse --short HEAD` when dealing with repositories containing tens of thousands of packfiles—a scenario common in Microsoft Git Scalar’s shared cache architecture. Johannes Schindelin identified the regression, which was introduced in October 2025 (commit `589127caa730`) and undid a prior fix from 2019. The issue stems from `packfile_store_add_pack()` performing a linear scan of the packfile list before each insertion, leading to real-world slowdowns from 0.4s to 4.5s in affected environments.

Johannes proposed a targeted fix that adds a fast-path optimization to skip duplicate checks for packfiles known to be new, eliminating the O(N²) behavior in the most common case. Junio C Hamano reviewed the patch and endorsed its design as "clever and clean," effectively approving it. However, Patrick Steinhardt raised a substantive alternative: replacing the linked-list implementation of `packfile_list` with a hashmap to make duplicate checks O(1) and enable future optimizations like O(log N) reordering. Johannes responded by acknowledging the technical merit of Patrick’s approach but defending the simplicity of his fast-path fix, arguing that the added complexity of a hashmap isn’t justified for the immediate regression. The design debate remains unresolved, with both solutions technically sound but differing in scope and future-proofing.

The patch is still under review, with no merge decision yet. Key participants include Junio (maintainer, approving the fast-path design), Peff (confirming the regression’s severity and clarifying test coverage), and Ben Knoble (whose question about `git maintenance` as a mitigation was resolved as unreliable for Scalar’s use case). The thread also highlights gaps in GitLab’s CI benchmarking, which failed to catch the regression due to flakiness and lack of coverage for many-pack scenarios. The unresolved question is whether the project will opt for Johannes’s minimal fix or Patrick’s more ambitious refactoring.
