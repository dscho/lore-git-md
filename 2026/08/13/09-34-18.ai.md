This is a **refactoring** discussion in the ongoing debate over Patrick Steinhardt’s RFC series to reorganize Git’s source tree by moving `libgit.a` components into a new `lib/` directory. Johannes Schindelin’s reply reinforces the pro-reorganization camp’s stance, framing the current disorganized source tree as an "ongoing burden" that outweighs the one-time cost of the patch. He argues that the cleaned-up root directory—even without further classification—improves discoverability for newcomers by making entry points like `builtin/` and `Documentation/` more visible, and dismisses critics’ objections as short-sighted.

Schindelin advances the thread by **doubling down on the incremental improvement argument**, positioning the `lib/` move as a necessary first step toward deeper reorganization. He acknowledges that naming and classification are hard (citing past refactorings like ODB and merge machinery as examples) and that the initial `lib/` structure may not be perfect, but insists that the change is worth merging now to enable future refinements. His third point is particularly pointed: he equates the maintenance pain of integrating this patch with other in-flight topics in `seen` to the *ongoing* friction caused by Git’s lack of tooling for handling mass renames (e.g., `git log --follow`), suggesting that the patch’s disruption is a feature, not a bug—it forces the community to confront this gap.

The reply also **amplifies Patrick’s "entry points" rationale**, agreeing that the value lies in making non-library code (e.g., `RelNotes`, `builtin/`) more discoverable rather than organizing the `lib/` directory itself. Schindelin’s example of the `RelNotes` symlink—a file many casual readers overlook—underscores his broader point: a tidier root directory reduces cognitive load for newcomers. However, he does not address Junio’s **structural critique** (that `lib/` is an arbitrary intermediate layer) or the **long-term mental overhead** of file moves, leaving the core deadlock intact. His focus on tooling improvements (e.g., better rename detection) as a *future* benefit also sidesteps whether such changes are realistic or who would implement them.

Key technical details for follow-up discussion:

- **Incrementalism**: Schindelin argues that the patch is a necessary first step, with further reorganization (e.g., splitting `lib/`) to follow later.
- **Tooling gap**: The patch’s disruption is positioned as a catalyst for improving Git’s handling of mass renames (e.g., `git log --follow`).
