This is a **substantive review** of the first patch in Derrick Stolee’s seven-part trace2 hardening series. Jeff King (Peff) engages directly with the architectural design of `banned-die.h`, questioning whether compile-time bans are the right tool for preventing `die()`-triggering behavior in trace2.

Peff’s core point is that `banned-die.h` treats `die()` like `strcpy()`—a function whose mere presence in the source code is the problem—when in reality the issue is the *runtime effect* of crashing during `atexit()` handlers. Because `die()` can be called transitively (e.g., `foo()` calls `bar()` which calls `die()`), a compile-time ban will miss many real failure modes, reducing the mechanism to a game of "whack-a-mole" as new `die()`-prone helpers are discovered and added to the ban list.

He suggests an alternative: a runtime flag that suppresses `die()`’s no-return behavior when trace2 is active. However, he acknowledges this is impractical because `die()`’s callers expect it to terminate the process, not return. His conclusion is that the whack-a-mole approach is the best available option, but he cautions against extending this pattern to other subsystems where runtime solutions would be more robust.

The review is technical and forward-looking, not a rejection of the series’ goal. It surfaces a fundamental tension between compile-time enforcement and runtime safety, and Peff’s framing implies that `banned-die.h` may need to be documented as a pragmatic but imperfect solution. No specific changes are demanded, but the critique could influence how future patches in the series are justified or how the header’s scope is communicated. The tone is measured and collaborative, not adversarial.
