This is a **substantive review** of the first patch in Derrick Stolee’s seven-part hardening series for the trace2 subsystem. Jeff King (Peff) engages deeply with the architectural implications of the new `banned-die.h` header, raising a fundamental concern: the mechanism’s effectiveness is inherently limited because it only catches *direct* calls to banned functions (like `die()`), not *transitive* calls (where a helper function like `foo()` calls `die()` internally). This creates a whack-a-mole problem—every function that might call `die()` would need to be banned, which is impractical and unscalable.

Peff acknowledges that the approach is likely the best available given Git’s constraints, particularly around `die()`’s no-return behavior, which makes runtime suppression difficult. However, he cautions against extending this strategy to other areas, where runtime flags or context-aware APIs might be more robust. His tone is pragmatic rather than dismissive, and he doesn’t oppose the patch outright, but his feedback signals a potential long-term maintainability issue if the mechanism becomes a template for broader hardening efforts.

The review doesn’t block the series—it’s more of a philosophical note for future consideration—but it’s a critical observation for anyone evaluating the patch’s architectural soundness. If the project later adopts `banned-die.h` for other subsystems (as Junio hinted in earlier replies), Peff’s concern about transitive calls could resurface as a real problem. For now, the patch remains a targeted fix for trace2’s `atexit()` fragility, but its broader applicability is questionable.
