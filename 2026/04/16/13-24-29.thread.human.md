Patrick Steinhardt’s RFC patch series to reorganize Git’s source tree by moving core files into a new `lib/` directory continues to spark debate, with Junio C Hamano now weighing in to reinforce Phillip Wood’s critique. The series, now at v3, remains a refactoring-only effort aimed at reducing root-directory clutter and establishing a clearer boundary for Git’s core library code. While Patrick frames the disruption to `git log --follow`, rebasing, and downstream forks as one-time costs, Junio’s latest reply underscores the *ongoing* friction of the change, particularly for maintainers working with the `seen` branch. His example—`git show origin/seen:wt-status.c` failing due to the rename—illustrates the recurring mental overhead of remembering file moves, a point he and Phillip both argue outweighs the structural benefits.

The core debate still hinges on whether the reorganization’s advantages (e.g., enforcing coding conventions like avoiding `the_repository` or `die()` in library code) justify the disruption. Junio and Phillip both dismiss the discoverability argument as weak, noting that tools like `git grep` or `ctags` already solve this problem, and instead propose non-structural solutions like improved documentation or LLM-generated code maps. Junio’s tone suggests he sees the change as more trouble than it’s worth, especially during the prerelease freeze, though he hasn’t outright rejected it. Meanwhile, supporters like Johannes Schindelin (who fixed Windows CI) and Oswald Buddenhagen continue to prioritize structural cleanup over logistical concerns.

The discussion now centers on two unresolved questions: (1) whether the coding-convention benefits are compelling enough to accept the ongoing friction, and (2) whether the project should explore alternative ways to improve discoverability without disrupting file locations. With Junio’s feedback adding weight to the opposition, the series may need further iteration—or a stronger case for its benefits—to gain traction. For now, the debate remains a clash between structural ideals and practical workflow costs.
