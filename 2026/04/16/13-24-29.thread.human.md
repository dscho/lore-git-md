Patrick Steinhardt’s RFC to reorganize Git’s source tree by moving core components into a new `lib/` directory remains a contentious refactoring effort aimed at improving discoverability for newcomers. The debate is still polarized, with proponents like Johannes Schindelin and brian m. carlson arguing that the one-time disruption is justified by long-term gains in codebase navigation, while critics such as Junio C Hamano and Taylor Blau emphasize the ongoing mental overhead of file moves and the structural flaws of the `lib/` approach. Junio’s recent proposal—a subsystem-by-subsystem reorganization—has sharpened the deadlock, offering a more incremental alternative that avoids the `lib/` directory’s ambiguity.

Now, Michael Montalbo, a newer contributor, has introduced a data-driven counterproposal that bridges the gap between these positions. Instead of a sweeping move to `lib/` or waiting for ad-hoc subsystem changes, Montalbo suggests using mechanical signals—specifically, shared internal header usage—to identify cohesive groups of files that could be moved together. His analysis, generated by a script, proposes five new subsystems (wire protocol, index, revision, diff, and pack) with higher cohesion scores than existing directories like `refs/` or `odb/`. This approach aims to address Junio’s critique of `lib/`’s arbitrariness while still enabling timely progress, offering a principled roadmap for reorganization. The proposal is pragmatic, focusing on project consistency rather than newcomer discoverability, and could shift the debate toward *how* to reorganize rather than *whether*.

The discussion remains unresolved, with key questions still open: Can Montalbo’s methodology gain traction, or will its novelty limit its adoption? Will the community accept header-sharing as a proxy for conceptual cohesion? And how will Junio’s and Taylor’s maintainer perspectives influence the outcome? For now, the thread is at an impasse, with no clear consensus on the best path forward.
