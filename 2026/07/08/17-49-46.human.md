This is a performance optimization patch series (v3) for Git's priority queue implementation, part of the ongoing effort to squeeze out small but measurable speedups in core data structures. The series targets the `prio_queue_get()` operation, replacing the standard sift-down algorithm with a more efficient cascade-down approach for cases where the queue isn't immediately refilled.

The v3 revision splits the change into two clean commits: first extracting the `sift_up()` helper from `prio_queue_put()` (a refactoring with no behavior change), then introducing the cascade optimization for unfused `prio_queue_get()` calls. This addresses earlier feedback about keeping the existing `sift_down_root()` logic intact for the fused replace path, where the new element might belong near the root. The optimization now only applies to the narrower case of removing the root without an immediate replacement, which is exactly where the cascade approach shines.

Benchmarks show the change delivers a modest but consistent ~2% end-to-end improvement in real-world scenarios like `rev-list --count` on repositories with thousands of active branches. The gain comes from reducing the number of comparisons per level from two to one during the extract-min operation, with the displaced element typically requiring only minimal sift-up work afterward. René Scharfe's exhaustive testing of all heap permutations up to 12 items confirms the cascade approach never requires more comparisons than standard sift-down, and the patch includes updated measurements across ascending, descending, and random insertion patterns showing no regressions.

The implementation is minimal (43 lines changed across two commits) and well-contained within `prio-queue.c`, making it a low-risk performance win. The series has been rebased on top of the recently merged `kk/prio-queue-get-put-fusion` work, which resolved earlier concerns about `prio_queue_replace()` regressions by fusing get+put operations. This leaves the cascade optimization to focus solely on the remaining unfused cases where it provides clear benefit.

Kristofer notes in the cover letter that both the performance gain and code cost are small, but given the thorough analysis and testing from René, he wanted to complete this iteration for proper discussion. The patch looks technically sound and addresses all previous feedback, though its ultimate fate may hinge on whether the maintainer considers the 1-2% improvement worth the additional complexity.
