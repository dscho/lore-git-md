This is a **substantive review follow-up** in the ongoing `no-ref-delta` capability thread. Jeff King (Peff) presses Taylor Blau on the technical justification for the feature, questioning whether the proposed `no-ref-delta` capability is the right solution to the problem it aims to solve.

Peff’s key concern is that the current design may be over-engineered for the stated use case—a write-through proxy that spools incoming packs byte-for-byte before replaying them upstream. He asks whether the proxy truly avoids indexing the pack (implying it does not know which OIDs are present) or merely defers indexing to an asynchronous single-pass process. If the latter, Peff argues that `REF_DELTA` entries should not be significantly more expensive to handle than `OFS_DELTA` entries, since both require random access to the pack and in-memory structures to resolve delta chains. The real bottleneck, he suggests, is not `REF_DELTA` itself but the possibility of delta bases appearing *after* their dependents in the pack, which can stall resolution until the base is encountered.

Peff proposes an alternative: a tighter protocol capability that enforces a stricter ordering constraint—bases before deltas—rather than outright banning `REF_DELTA`. This would address the worst-case scenario (long delta chains stalling resolution) while preserving flexibility for clients that can handle `REF_DELTA`. His critique is technical and grounded in Git’s pack protocol principles, but it also reflects a broader unease about adding niche optimizations without clear, generalizable benefits.

The exchange leaves the series at a crossroads. Taylor’s original motivation (avoiding `REF_DELTA` to simplify asynchronous indexing) is now better understood, but Peff’s questions suggest the design may need refinement—or at least a more compelling justification—to align with Git’s protocol philosophy.
