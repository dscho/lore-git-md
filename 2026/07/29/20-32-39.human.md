This is a follow-up in the ongoing RFC discussion about allowing Git to accept packfiles with duplicate objects. Junio C Hamano responds to Taylor Blau’s defense of the feature, clarifying his position and outlining a longer-term vision for how Git should handle duplicates.

Junio acknowledges that he could accept an extension where the receiving end deduplicates objects during `index-pack`, but he remains firmly opposed to storing packs with duplicates on disk. His concern is that allowing such packs to persist would risk spreading "broken" repositories, creating a maintenance burden for the broader ecosystem. He treats this as a fundamental principle: Git should never store invalid packfiles, even if it can safely process them in memory.

To address the performance use case Taylor described (e.g., concatenating packs to avoid deduplication overhead), Junio suggests that the receiving end should deduplicate objects *during* indexing, rather than accepting them as-is. This would preserve the performance benefit of streaming packs without violating Git’s integrity guarantees. He acknowledges that this would require additional tooling to "fix" repositories by reindexing, but frames it as a necessary trade-off to prevent long-term technical debt.

Junio also agrees with Taylor’s observation that Git already tolerates duplicates in non-strict `index-pack` and shallow/filtered clones, calling this a "sensible longer-term direction" to reject duplicates in *all* modes, including those paths. He implies that the current RFC should not be merged as-is, but could be revisited if it were redesigned to deduplicate objects during indexing. This aligns with his earlier philosophical objection: Git should enforce correctness at the earliest possible stage, not defer it to later cleanup.

The email leaves the thread at a crossroads. Taylor’s proposed design (allowing duplicates as an opt-in exception) is now explicitly at odds with Junio’s vision (deduplicating during indexing). The ball is back in Taylor’s court to either propose a new approach that aligns with Junio’s direction or argue why the performance trade-offs justify an exception. The discussion has shifted from "whether" to "how," but the fundamental tension between performance and correctness remains unresolved.
