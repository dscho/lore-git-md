This is a **substantive discussion** about Git’s recoverability limitations, sparked by the `git history squash` feature but addressing a broader UX gap. Patrick Steinhardt responds to Phillip Wood’s critique of Git’s reflog by expanding on the systemic problem and proposing a long-term solution.

Patrick **agrees with Phillip’s assessment** that Git’s reflog is inadequate for undoing multi-ref operations like `git history squash --update-refs`. He frames the issue as a **fundamental mismatch** between the reflog’s design and user needs: it’s simultaneously *too detailed* (logging individual ref updates when users care about high-level operations) and *not detailed enough* (lacking context about what the operation *was*). This echoes Harald Nordgren’s earlier proposal for a "human-centered reflog" with visual hierarchy, but Patrick takes it further by suggesting a concrete architectural addition: an **operation log (oplog)**.

The oplog idea is **forward-looking and ambitious**. Patrick envisions it as a way to log high-level operations (e.g., "squashed commits A..B") alongside their constituent ref updates, enabling commands like `git undo` to revert entire operations atomically. This would address the recoverability gap Matt Hunter and Phillip Wood highlighted, where `git reset --hard` fails to undo `--update-refs` because it doesn’t know which branches were moved. Patrick notes that tools like Jujutsu (`jj`) and git-branchless already implement similar functionality, and he **explicitly invites contributions** to explore this direction.

### Key takeaways

- The recoverability issue is **not specific to `git history squash`**—it’s a systemic limitation of Git’s reflog design.
- An oplog could enable **atomic undo** of multi-ref operations, but it would require significant architectural changes.
- Patrick’s tone is **pragmatic**: he acknowledges this is a long-term goal ("it'll take a while") and encourages others to drive the effort.

This reply **shifts the thread from feature-specific feedback to a broader discussion** about Git’s UX. While the `squash` series itself is ready for final review, the recoverability critique may inspire future work on Git’s tooling. For now, users will need to rely on manual recovery or `--dry-run` to preview changes.
