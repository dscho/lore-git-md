This is a **performance optimization follow-up** for the `git last-modified` command, part of an ongoing thread about its speed relative to a hand-rolled `git log` pipeline. Toon Claes, the command’s author, responds to Jeff King’s (Peff) earlier analysis and proof-of-concept patch that demonstrated a 4× speedup by reusing Bloom filter logic from `revision.c`.

Toon acknowledges the performance gap Peff identified—where `git last-modified` inefficiently checks every path in a directory against every commit’s Bloom filter—and confirms that Peff’s approach (pre-filtering commits with a single pathspec-derived Bloom key) aligns with his own experiments. He shares additional benchmarks from GitLab’s repositories, showing consistent improvements: a 1.56× speedup on the `gitlab-org/gitlab` monorepo (where the `app/` directory is touched in 35% of commits) and a 1.33× improvement on `gitlab-org/gitaly` (58% of commits touch `internal/`). Even recursive queries benefit, with a 1.13× speedup. These results suggest the optimization holds across different repository shapes and commit patterns, addressing Peff’s concern about potential regressions in frequently modified directories.

Toon also clarifies his priorities: while Peff’s patch handles literal pathspecs (the common case), Toon defers non-literal pathspec support (e.g., `*.c`) as out of scope for now. He plans to coordinate with Peff to merge their efforts into a single patch series, noting that Peff’s suggestion to refactor `revision.c` internals (rather than exposing them directly) is the right path forward. The patch itself touches `builtin/last-modified.c`, `revision.c`, and `revision.h`, but introduces no user-visible changes—it’s purely an internal optimization leveraging existing commit-graph and Bloom filter infrastructure.

The thread remains under active review, with no integration branch yet. The next steps are clear: Toon will drive a unified patch series, incorporating Peff’s approach while addressing the refactoring and testing gaps Peff flagged. The benchmarks and alignment between the two authors suggest this is a well-motivated, uncontroversial improvement that should land smoothly. For readers tracking Git’s performance work, this is a small but meaningful win—especially for tools like GitLab that batch queries—but not a disruptive change.
