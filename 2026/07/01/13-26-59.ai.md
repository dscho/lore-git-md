This is a **substantive review** of Patrick Steinhardt’s RFC patch (v2 2/2) to reorganize Git’s source tree by moving `libgit.a` components into a new `lib/` directory. Phillip Wood engages with the core trade-offs of the proposal, offering a nuanced critique of both the discoverability argument and the practical costs of the reorganization.

Phillip rejects the claim that the move improves discoverability, arguing that the number of files to search remains the same—just nested under an extra directory. He suggests that tools like `git grep` or `ctags` are better solutions for navigating the codebase, not structural reorganization. However, he endorses the idea of a dedicated `lib/` directory as a way to enforce coding conventions (e.g., avoiding `the_repository` or `die()` in library code), calling this a "much stronger selling point" than discoverability.

On the downsides, Phillip highlights a concrete friction point: the mental overhead of remembering that files have moved, illustrated by his own experience failing to locate `wt-status.c` in its new location (`lib/wt-status.c`). This echoes earlier concerns about disrupted workflows (e.g., `git log --follow`, rebasing) but frames it as a recurring annoyance rather than a one-time cost. His tone is constructive, focusing on trade-offs rather than outright opposition.

The review advances the thread by:
1. **Challenging the discoverability rationale** while offering an alternative justification (coding conventions).
2. **Adding a new practical cost** (mental overhead of tracking file moves) to the list of downsides.
3. **Reinforcing the need for tooling improvements** (e.g., better `git log --follow` support) to mitigate disruption.

Key technical details mentioned: `lib/wt-status.c`, `git log --follow`, `git grep`, `ctags`, `the_repository`, `die()`. The review does not propose specific changes to the patch but shifts the debate toward whether the structural benefits (e.g., enforcing library conventions) justify the ongoing friction. Likely points of contention: whether the coding-convention argument is compelling enough to outweigh the workflow disruption.
