This is a **refactoring** patch discussion, specifically a reply in the ongoing debate about Patrick Steinhardt’s RFC to move Git’s `libgit.a` sources into a new `lib/` directory. Phillip Wood weighs in with a mixed but pragmatic perspective.

Phillip pushes back on the "discoverability" argument that Patrick and others have used to justify the reorganization. He argues that moving files into `lib/` doesn’t actually make the codebase easier to navigate—there are still just as many files to search through, just one more directory to check. Instead of structural changes, he suggests that better tooling (like `git grep` or ctags) is the real solution to discoverability issues. This aligns with the skepticism voiced by Junio and SZEDER Gábor in earlier replies.

However, Phillip does see a concrete benefit in the reorganization: it creates a clearer boundary for Git’s core library code. By grouping all library files in one place, it becomes easier to enforce coding standards specific to library code—like avoiding `the_repository` or preferring `error()` over `die()`. This is a more targeted and practical argument than the broad "discoverability" claim, and it may resonate with contributors focused on code hygiene and long-term maintainability.

Phillip also highlights a real-world cost of the change: the mental overhead of remembering where files have moved. He shares a personal anecdote about struggling to find `wt-status.c` after it was relocated to `lib/wt-status.c`, illustrating how the reorganization can disrupt familiar workflows. This is a tangible example of the "one-time cost" Patrick acknowledges, and it reinforces the concerns about disruption to ongoing work.

The tone is constructive and measured. Phillip doesn’t outright reject the proposal but instead reframes the debate around more specific, actionable benefits (library code boundaries) while acknowledging the downsides (workflow disruption). His feedback is unlikely to settle the debate, but it adds a nuanced perspective that could help shape the next iteration of the series—if Patrick chooses to revise it further. For now, the discussion remains polarized between structural benefits and logistical costs.
