This is the final patch in a **performance optimization series** for Git’s merge-base calculation, targeting the `paint_down_to_common()` function in the commit-reach subsystem. The series is **ready to land** and has broad reviewer endorsement (Derrick Stolee, Elijah Newren, and the original author, Tian Yuchen).

This patch implements the **core optimization**: an early-exit condition in `paint_down_to_common()` that terminates the walk when one side of the history is exhausted, eliminating unnecessary traversal of large one-sided histories. The optimization activates only after entering the finite-generation region (where generation numbers guarantee topological ordering), ensuring correctness. Benchmarks show **100-1000x speedups** for asymmetric queries (e.g., `7.26s → 88ms` for a 2.6M-commit monorepo), and trace2 instrumentation confirms the reduction in steps walked (e.g., `80 → 9` in git.git).

The patch updates the technical documentation (`Documentation/technical/paint-down-to-common.adoc`) to explain the new "side-exhaustion" termination condition, clarifying why it’s safe to exit early when one side’s exclusive counter reaches zero and no pending merge-base candidates remain. The code change itself is minimal: the early-exit check is added to `paint_queue_get()`, gated by `commit_graph_generation(commit) < GENERATION_NUMBER_INFINITY` to avoid misfires in the INFINITY region. The test in `t6600-test-reach.sh` is updated to reflect the new step counts, validating the optimization’s impact.

This is a **well-motivated, thoroughly tested** improvement with no regressions or on-disk format changes. The series has resolved all feedback from v1, including structural disputes (preserving `nonstale_queue` for `ahead_behind()`), finalizing the termination condition, and encapsulating state in the `paint_state` struct. The commit message includes concrete benchmarks and edge-case reasoning, and the test coverage is comprehensive. If you care about Git’s performance in large repositories—especially those with shallow histories or import grafts—this is worth your attention.
