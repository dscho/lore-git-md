This is **Patrick Steinhardt’s reply** to Jeff King’s substantive review of the recursive initialization issue in the ref backend series. It marks a **significant shift in the series’ direction**, abandoning the v2/v3 "onbranch" exclusion workaround in favor of a deeper architectural redesign.

Patrick acknowledges Peff’s critique that the current approach—selectively blocking "onbranch" config includes during ref store initialization—is a band-aid rather than a root-cause fix. He now agrees the real problem lies in the ref backends’ premature parsing of write-related config (e.g., `core.logAllRefUpdates`, `core.sharedRepository`) during initialization, when these settings are only needed for writes. This insight reframes the solution: instead of tweaking config parsing to avoid recursion, the backends should **defer all write-config parsing until the first write operation**.

The reply highlights a key technical hurdle: while the "files" backend could adopt lazy-loading with minimal changes, the "reftable" backend’s current design tightly couples write-config parsing with stack construction, requiring a major refactor. Patrick identifies this as a design flaw—the reftable stack should only need read-critical config (e.g., hash algorithm) at initialization, with write options passed dynamically later. This aligns with Peff’s suggestion to minimize early config loading and defer non-critical settings to uncached paths like `repo_config()`.

The tone is pragmatic but self-critical ("I really shouldn’t have opened this can of worms"), signaling a likely **v4 rewrite** of patches 7–9/10. The new approach would:
1. Split backend initialization into "read-only" and "write-ready" phases.
2. Move all write-config parsing out of `get_main_ref_store()` and into write operations.
3. Refactor the reftable backend to decouple stack construction from write options.

This redesign addresses Peff’s concerns about config cache inconsistencies and user-facing regressions (e.g., `onbranch`-scoped `core.logAllRefUpdates` not being respected) while avoiding the "hacky" feel of the v3 workaround. However, it introduces new complexity, particularly for the reftable backend, and may delay the series’ merge timeline. The shift also underscores the broader challenge of balancing ref backend initialization with Git’s config system—a tension likely to recur in future backend work.

For reviewers, this reply raises the stakes: the next iteration will require careful scrutiny of the lazy-loading design, especially its impact on the reftable backend’s architecture and performance. The thread’s focus has moved from a narrow recursion fix to a foundational question about how ref backends should interact with config, making this a critical juncture for the series.
