The worktree diff performance thread has taken a turn toward a simpler fix after Jeff King (Peff) demonstrated a working patch that forces index refreshes by treating racy entries as stat-dirty. Junio C Hamano has now suggested stepping back from architectural changes to the diff machinery and instead leveraging nanosecond timestamp precision (`USE_NSEC`) to eliminate racy entries at their source. His proposal is to delay index writes by 50 milliseconds when nanosecond resolution is available, which would naturally prevent the timestamp collisions that trigger full content checks in secondary worktrees. This approach sidesteps the complexity of Peff’s patch—including its first-diff performance regression—and the long-debated counter-based guard in `diffcore_skip_stat_unmatch()`.

The thread began with D. Ben Knoble’s investigation into why `git diff` is dramatically slower in secondary worktrees (223ms vs 3ms in the main worktree), which traced the issue to racy-git scenarios where second-granularity timestamps force Git to re-check file content. Peff’s patch addressed this by ensuring racy entries increment the counter, triggering an index refresh that persists across subsequent diffs, but at the cost of a 3.6x slowdown on the first diff. Junio’s alternative reframes the problem as a timestamp precision issue rather than an index refresh one, proposing that nanosecond timestamps—already supported in the codebase—could be the most direct solution. This shifts the discussion from runtime fixes and architectural tradeoffs to a build-time or platform-specific solution, though it leaves open questions about how to handle platforms where nanosecond precision isn’t available or where `USE_NSEC` isn’t enabled.

The key participants remain aligned on the core problem but divided on the best path forward. Peff has focused on optimizing his patch to address the first-diff slowdown, while Junio’s suggestion implies a preference for avoiding complexity in the diff machinery. D. Ben Knoble and brian m. carlson, who had previously advocated for runtime configuration and user control, may now need to reconcile their approaches with Junio’s timestamp-centric proposal. The thread is now at a crossroads: whether to proceed with Peff’s patch (and its tradeoffs), adopt Junio’s timestamp-based fix, or explore a hybrid solution that combines the two. The outcome will likely hinge on further performance measurements and whether the project prioritizes architectural simplicity or runtime flexibility.
