Matt **endorses Phillip Wood’s usability critique** of the `--reedit-message` template, agreeing that omitting fully commented-out `fixup!` messages (while retaining a summary list of commit subjects at the top) would reduce visual clutter. They draw a parallel to `git rebase -i`, where the todo list provides similar context, and suggest this approach would confirm which commits are included in the squash range without overwhelming the editor. Their feedback is narrowly focused on the template’s readability and does not challenge the core design.

On **`squash!` messages**, Matt asks whether they should be treated differently from `fixup!` messages (which are omitted entirely). This is a **clarification question** rather than a design proposal, but it surfaces a subtle usability point: users might expect `squash!` messages to contribute content to the final commit, unlike `fixup!` messages, which are typically discarded.

Matt’s most substantive observation is a **surprise at `--reedit-message` not being the default**. They frame this as a workflow question: if `git history squash` is often followed by `git history reword` to clean up the commit message, why not make re-editing the default? This touches on a broader design choice for the `git history` suite—whether it prioritizes speed (defaulting to automatic behavior) or safety (defaulting to user confirmation). Their question implies that users accustomed to `git rebase -i`’s interactive flow might expect similar behavior here.

Finally, Matt raises a **recoverability concern**: if a `git history` operation goes wrong, is there a foolproof way to undo it? They note that `git reset --hard` might not suffice if `--update-refs` moved other branches, hinting at a potential usability gap for users who rely on branch pointers to track state. This is not a critique of the squash feature specifically but a broader question about the `git history` suite’s safety net.
