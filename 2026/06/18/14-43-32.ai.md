This is a **substantive review** of Junio C Hamano’s documentation patch, focusing on the project’s contribution guidelines. Kristoffer Haugsbakk engages critically with the proposed requirement to justify design decisions in resubmissions, arguing that the current patch—and the project’s broader stance on design-only proposals—may discourage valuable early feedback from contributors who lack the time or expertise to implement their ideas.

Haugsbakk’s core objection centers on the tension between the patch’s call for upfront design justification and the project’s historical practice of dismissing design-only emails with a "show me the code" response. He illustrates this with a hypothetical contributor who proposes a feature (e.g., a new option) but hesitates to implement it without knowing whether the design would be accepted. The project’s standard reply—"submit some code, even if incomplete"—strikes him as misaligned with the patch’s stated goal of avoiding wasted effort on unsound designs. He suggests that this policy disproportionately burdens contributors who are not experienced C developers, while offering little benefit to the project, since design flaws in such proposals are often obvious to seasoned Git users.

The review also highlights a cultural divide: the patch assumes contributors can readily defend their designs in writing, but Haugsbakk notes that many users (e.g., "partisan Git users" who lack C experience) may struggle to articulate their ideas in terms the project finds acceptable. He proposes a softer alternative—replacing "would this be accepted?" with "could this be accepted?"—to encourage speculative design discussions without requiring immediate implementation. His critique extends to the project’s unofficial idea tracker (GitGitGadget), which he finds underutilized for this purpose.

While Haugsbakk praises the clarity of the patch’s language (e.g., "it is now clear why we may want to do this"), his broader argument questions whether the patch’s prescriptive tone will achieve its intended goal. The review is **not a rejection** of the patch but a call to reconcile its requirements with the project’s existing barriers to entry. No technical alternatives are proposed, but the feedback suggests the patch might benefit from acknowledging the practical challenges of design-first contributions.

Key terms introduced or referenced: *design-only proposals*, *GitGitGadget idea tracker*, *canned response* ("make us come to you, begging"). The discussion touches on governance norms but remains focused on contributor workflows rather than code or policy mechanics.
