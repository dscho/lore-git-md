**Technical discussion** (reply) — Peff weighs in on Taylor Blau's proposed `reassign_tempfile_ownership()` solution for the daemonization locking bug, endorsing the general direction while raising a subtle atomicity concern with the implementation.

Peff opens by pushing back on Taylor's characterization of the "drop and re-acquire" alternative as unworkably racy. He points out that `git gc` already uses exactly that pattern — it explicitly deletes the lockfile before daemonizing — and that the only real risk is starvation, which is both unlikely and self-limiting (the final invoker will always win the race eventually). This is a useful calibration: Peff is not arguing against Taylor's proposed fix, but noting that the problem it solves is a correctness and design hygiene issue, not an absolute hard requirement.

The core of the reply is Peff's answer to Taylor's question about whether all tempfiles should be reassigned on fork. Peff argues *yes, all of them*, and his reasoning is worth quoting: "daemonize() is not about spawning a child... There is still a single contiguous flow of execution, and the fact that we switch pids and call exit() is an implementation detail." From that perspective, every tempfile and lockfile should follow the new process id, because there isn't really a "parent" and "child" — there's just one logical execution that happens to change pid. Peff extends the argument to atexit handlers in general, noting that if we had other running resources (e.g. child processes from `run_processes_parallel()`) we'd want similar treatment, but that situation doesn't arise in practice because daemonize is called early.

The most significant technical contribution is Peff's analysis of a race condition in Taylor's implementation. The proposed patch has a window during which both the parent and child could claim ownership (after the child takes ownership but before the parent finishes reassigning), or neither could (if the parent relinquishes before the child takes). Peff works through the failure modes: the double-claim scenario can, in an elaborate sequence involving signals and a third process, cause a lock to be deleted out from under someone who just acquired it. The no-claim scenario just leaves a stale lock behind, which is annoying but not worse than a `kill -9`. He offers no easy fix — synchronizing between parent and child with pipes would add significant complexity — and seems to conclude that the race is unlikely enough to be acceptable.

This is a characteristically sharp Peff review: he validates the overall design, clarifies the motivation for why it should apply universally, and then drills down to a real but probably tolerable edge case that the author may not have considered. The implication for the thread is that Taylor's solution is likely workable with minor polish, but the window between the two `reassign_tempfile_ownership()` calls may need to be made atomic or at least narrower, perhaps by having the parent reassign first and the child take over afterward, rather than having both directions in flight simultaneously.
